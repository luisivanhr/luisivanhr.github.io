<section class="intro-strip" id="question"><h2>When the posterior integral is difficult</h2><p>Lesson 23 normalized a simple posterior by hand. A larger copula model can have several dependence and marginal-distribution parameters, leaving a posterior that is easy to evaluate up to a constant and hard to integrate. [[Markov chain Monte Carlo::MCMC]] constructs dependent draws whose long-run distribution is the target posterior. Shemyakin and Kniazev apply Metropolis sampling to Bayesian copula estimation in Example 7.3.6. <a class="course-citation" href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a> This lesson develops the mechanism before the numerical workshop.</p></section>

<p>A [[Markov chain]] is a sequence whose next-state distribution depends on its current state rather than its whole history. Here the state is a parameter value. The chain's iteration number is a computational index; it does not represent observation time in the copula model. <a class="course-citation" href="#ref-2" aria-label="Reference 2">[2]</a></p>

<h2 id="target">The target needs only relative weights</h2>
<p>Suppose observations \(D=\{(u_i,v_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) are independent conditional on a copula parameter \(\theta\), and the continuous marginal distributions are known. Their transformed coordinates then have a copula-density likelihood \(L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n c_\theta(u_i,v_i)\). With prior density \(\pi_0(\theta)\) supported on a parameter domain \(\Theta\), Bayes' rule gives the target below.</p>
<div class="math-block definition"><span class="block-label">Definition 24.1 <span>Unnormalized posterior target</span></span><p>Set \(f(\theta)=L(\theta)\pi_0(\theta)\) for \(\theta\in\Theta\), and \(f(\theta)=0\) outside \(\Theta\). If \(Z=\int_\Theta f(t)\,dt\) is finite and positive, the posterior density is \(p(\theta\mid D)=f(\theta)/Z\). A Metropolis–Hastings acceptance ratio uses values of \(f\); the unknown \(Z\) cancels. <a class="course-citation" href="#ref-2" aria-label="Reference 2">[2]</a></p></div>
<p>Knowing the marginal distributions makes this an exact likelihood for the transformed observations. If the marginal distributions are estimated from these same data, their uncertainty requires treatment in a full Bayesian model. Treating sample ranks as independent exact copula draws instead produces a pseudo-likelihood. Example 7.3.6 distinguishes a two-step marginal fit from a simultaneous parameter update. <a class="course-citation" href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">[1]</a></p>

<h2 id="metropolis-hastings">Proposal, acceptance, and a possible repeat</h2>
<p>Choose a current state \(\theta\in\Theta\) with \(f(\theta)>0\). A proposal density \(q(y\mid\theta)\) gives the density of proposing value \(y\) from current state \(\theta\). It must be possible to evaluate both \(q(y\mid\theta)\) and the reverse density \(q(\theta\mid y)\) wherever the algorithm may move. Propose \(Y=y\) and draw an independent \(R\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)\). Move to \(y\) when \(R\le\alpha(\theta,y)\); otherwise retain \(\theta\), recording the repeated state. The source gives this general algorithm and its ratio in equation (4.3). <a class="course-citation" href="#ref-2" aria-label="Reference 2">[2]</a></p>
<div class="math-block theorem"><span class="block-label">Rule 24.2 <span>Metropolis–Hastings acceptance</span></span><p>For a valid proposed state \(y\in\Theta\) with positive forward proposal density,</p><p>\[\alpha(\theta,y)=\min\!\left\{1,\frac{f(y)q(\theta\mid y)}{f(\theta)q(y\mid\theta)}\right\}.\]</p><p>If \(f(y)=0\), including a proposal outside the parameter domain, reject it. A proposal with no reverse path also has acceptance probability zero. Rejection is a transition from \(\theta\) back to \(\theta\), not the deletion of an iteration.</p></div>
<p>The factor \(q(\theta\mid y)/q(y\mid\theta)\) corrects directional bias in the proposal. For a symmetric proposal, these terms cancel. A proposal concentrated near the current state can be convenient, but nearby proposed values may produce strongly correlated recorded states.</p>

<div class="math-block proof"><span class="block-label">Why the posterior is invariant</span><p>For distinct interior states \(x,y\), multiply the target weight by the density of an accepted move:</p><p>\[f(x)q(y\mid x)\alpha(x,y)=\min\{f(x)q(y\mid x),\,f(y)q(x\mid y)\}=f(y)q(x\mid y)\alpha(y,x).\]</p><p>Dividing both sides by \(Z\) gives [[detailed balance]] for the normalized posterior. The complete transition also assigns its leftover probability to staying at \(x\). That diagonal mass is balanced with itself. Integrating the off-diagonal identity over starting states, then including the diagonal term, shows that one step preserves \(p\) when the chain begins with distribution \(p\). This is invariance. It does not say that a chain started at an arbitrary value has already reached \(p\), nor does detailed balance alone establish convergence from every starting state. Convergence needs additional properties of the transition mechanism. The source uses detailed balance in its justification and separately discusses convergence and diagnostics. <a class="course-citation" href="#ref-2" aria-label="Reference 2">[2]</a></p></div>

<h2 id="random-walk">A symmetric random walk on a bounded parameter set</h2>
<p>For a scalar parameter, a common proposal is \(Y=\theta+\sigma\varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\) is standard normal and \(\sigma>0\). On the real line, \(q(y\mid\theta)=q(\theta\mid y)\), so the acceptance ratio reduces to \(f(y)/f(\theta)\). This is the [[random-walk Metropolis]] proposal in equation (4.5). <a class="course-citation" href="#ref-3" aria-label="Reference 3">[3]</a> If \(\Theta=[-1,1]\), retain the same untruncated normal proposal and reject values outside \([-1,1]\). Repeatedly drawing until a proposal lies inside the interval would produce a truncated proposal whose normalizing factor depends on the current state. The symmetry simplification would then need to be reconsidered.</p>
<p>Lesson 23 gives an exact benchmark from two known transformed FGM observations, \((1/4,1/4)\) and \((3/4,3/4)\), with a uniform prior on \([-1,1]\). Its unnormalized posterior target is \(f(\theta)=(1+\theta/4)^2\) inside the interval and zero outside; a constant prior factor can be dropped. The exact posterior mean is \(8/49\). These data and numbers are original course illustrations of the book's algorithm, not observations from Example 7.3.6. At current state \(0\), a proposal \(1/2\) has target ratio \(f(1/2)/f(0)=81/64\), so it is accepted. The reverse proposal has acceptance probability \(64/81\). The workshop will compare simulated estimates with this known answer.</p>

<h2 id="reading-output">Reading a chain carefully</h2>
<p>When the chain has the required convergence properties, averages of recorded states can estimate posterior expectations. An initial transient can affect finite runs. The recorded sequence is dependent: rejected proposals repeat values, and accepted nearby proposals can still be correlated. Choosing \(\sigma\) too small often yields high acceptance but slow movement across \(\Theta\). Choosing it too large can produce many low-weight or out-of-domain proposals and repeated states. Shemyakin and Kniazev illustrate both problems for normal random-walk proposals. <a class="course-citation" href="#ref-3" aria-label="Reference 3">[3]</a></p>
<p>A trace plot shows the order of visited values; a histogram shows their distribution but hides the order. Acceptance rate, a running mean, and sample autocorrelation add different views. Discarding an initial burn-in segment can reduce the effect of an arbitrary starting value, but its length cannot be certified by a fixed percentage. Agreement between chains started at separated values is useful evidence; visual agreement alone is not a proof of convergence. The source presents these diagnostics and their limits in Section 4.5. <a class="course-citation" href="#ref-4" aria-label="Reference 4">[4]</a> The exact FGM posterior lets the next lesson check simulated output against an analytical answer. In a realistic problem without an exact answer, diagnostics remain necessary and imperfect.</p>

<h2 id="exercises">Exercises</h2>
<article class="exercise"><header class="exercise-head"><div><strong>Exercise 24.1</strong><span>An asymmetric proposal</span></div><button class="answer-button" type="button">Show answer</button></header><div class="exercise-body"><p>Suppose \(f(y)/f(x)=2\) and \(q(x\mid y)/q(y\mid x)=1/4\). Find the acceptance probabilities for \(x\to y\) and \(y\to x\). Why would using only the target ratio be incorrect?</p></div><div class="answer-panel"><div class="answer-inner"><p>The forward ratio is \(2(1/4)=1/2\), so the acceptance probability is \(1/2\). The reverse ratio is \(2\), giving acceptance probability one. Using the target ratio alone would accept the forward move with probability one and omit the proposal's directional imbalance.</p></div></div></article>
<article class="exercise"><header class="exercise-head"><div><strong>Exercise 24.2</strong><span>Invariance without exploration</span></div><button class="answer-button" type="button">Show answer</button></header><div class="exercise-body"><p>Consider a chain that always retains its current state. Explain why any initial distribution is invariant. If the chain starts at zero, does this let it approximate the continuous FGM posterior?</p></div><div class="answer-panel"><div class="answer-inner"><p>The state never changes, so its distribution stays equal to its initial distribution after every step. In particular, a posterior-distributed initial state would retain that distribution. Starting at zero instead gives zero forever. The empirical distribution is a point mass, so invariance alone supplies no exploration or convergence from this initial state.</p></div></div></article>
<article class="exercise"><header class="exercise-head"><div><strong>Exercise 24.3</strong><span>Boundary and repeated state</span></div><button class="answer-button" type="button">Show answer</button></header><div class="exercise-body"><p>The current FGM parameter is \(0.9\), and the untruncated normal random walk proposes \(1.1\). What state is recorded? Would drawing repeatedly until a proposal lies in \([-1,1]\) preserve the symmetric proposal rule?</p></div><div class="answer-panel"><div class="answer-inner"><p>The target is zero at \(1.1\), so reject the proposal and record \(0.9\) again. This iteration counts. Repeated drawing until a proposal is inside produces a truncated normal distribution. Its normalizing probability depends on the current state, so the forward and reverse proposal densities generally differ. The acceptance ratio would need that correction.</p></div></div></article>
